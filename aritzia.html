Summary

I bought Aritzia because it sells clothes at the same prices as its rivals but wraps them in a far better experience, and that edge is showing up as growth the rest of the industry can't match. When I bought, the market was paying less for that growth than it had a few months earlier. And there is still plenty of room to grow, with fewer than half of its possible US stores built and menswear just getting started.

My thesis rests on four points:

  1. The experience is the moat. Comparable quality and prices to Lululemon, Alo and the elevated-basics brands, but a store visit that feels like luxury: big fitting rooms, lounges, Style Advisors, and an in-store café.
  2. The growth is undeniable. Revenue has grown at least 32% in each of the last six quarters, and adjusted EPS grew 64% last fiscal year.
  3. The price didn't follow the growth. The stock fell from C$174.52 to about C$115 while revenue accelerated. My average cost of C$122.91 is about 23x this year's expected earnings, a reasonable price even if growth slows to the 15–20% a year its five-year plan implies.
  4. The US is the runway, and men are next. Two-thirds of revenue now comes from the US, where Aritzia holds about 1% of the market and its sales grew 60% last quarter while Lululemon's Americas sales shrink. A dedicated menswear line opens a second customer it barely serves today.

The main risk: apparel is discretionary. In a recession, shoppers cut back on clothes first, and a stock priced for growth falls hard when growth slows.

The business

Aritzia is a Vancouver-based design house founded in 1984. It designs its own women's apparel brands (Wilfred, Babaton, TNA and others) and sells them only through its own boutiques, website and app, under the positioning it calls “Everyday Luxury.” Because it owns the product and the channel, it keeps the full margin and controls every part of how the brand is experienced.

01The experience is the moat

I've shopped at Lululemon, Alo and the workwear brands Aritzia competes with. The clothes are comparable in quality and price. The experience is not.

At Aritzia, the boutique is the product as much as the clothes. The stores have marble tables and staircases, oversized fitting rooms, and lounge seating where whoever you came with can wait. Staff check in on you, bring you ice cream, and help you put outfits together. In the larger stores there's the A-OK Café, and when I spent above a set amount I was given a coin for a free drink.

That drink costs Aritzia a few dollars at most, but it turns buying clothes into an outing. You walk into a room that feels expensive, then find the pieces are about C$70–80. That gap between how the store feels and what you pay is what keeps people coming back.

What the evidence says

  • The stores are built for it. The new 18,000 sq ft Toronto Eaton Centre flagship has marble stairs, two large dressing-room areas, lounge seating, an Atelier, in-store Style Advisors and an A-OK Café serving coffee, pastries and soft-serve (Retail Insider). Café drinks run C$4–6.50 (Black Press).
  • It draws traffic. Management says comparable-store growth has been driven mainly by more visits, and that a Florida opening with an A-OK Café had lineups around the door (Q2 FY26 call).
  • It keeps customers. Most clients are Gen Z and Millennials, and management says those who discover the brand young stay loyal for years. New US clients look like the existing loyal base.
  • It holds pricing. Adjusted gross margin rose 4.9 points to 48.7% last quarter. In Q1 the company credited higher initial markups and fewer markdowns. A brand that has to discount can't do that.

02The growth is undeniable

Quarter after quarter, Aritzia keeps growing faster than almost anyone in apparel.

  • Fiscal 2026 (year to Feb 2026): revenue +35% to C$3.70B, US revenue +44%, adjusted EPS +64% to C$3.25.
  • This year so far: revenue +43% in Q1 and +44% in Q2, with comparable sales up about 35% in both quarters.
  • Guidance raised twice: full-year revenue guidance went from C$4.4–4.6B in May to C$4.78–4.88B in October, 29–32% growth.
  • Profit grows faster than sales: adjusted EBITDA margin hit a Q2 record of 21.0%, up 5.9 points, as fixed costs spread over more revenue.

One item to adjust for: Q2 reported EPS of C$1.70 includes a one-time C$97.4M US tariff refund. I use adjusted EPS of C$1.31 (vs. C$0.59 a year ago).

03The price didn't follow the growth

From its June peak to early October, the stock fell about a third while the business posted its two fastest quarters of growth. That gap is why I bought.

Why I bought: the market cut Aritzia’s P/E from about 27x to 22x on fears that growth would slow, while earnings estimates held roughly steady (the P/E fell about as much as the price). The evidence pointed the other way: margins were rising with fewer markdowns, and US sales were growing 60%, with fewer than half of Aritzia’s possible US stores built.

The cushion: at my cost I paid about 23x this year’s expected earnings (~C$5.34, up from C$3.25). This year’s 64% jump is boosted by margins recovering, so I don’t lean on it. The five-year plan targets 15–17% revenue growth a year, and with buybacks and steady margins, earnings should grow somewhat faster. A company growing around 20% a year is usually valued at 20–25x, so even at that slower pace I wasn’t overpaying, and anything faster is upside.

04The US is the runway

Aritzia is taking market share in the US while the biggest names in apparel stall, and it has barely started building stores there.

  • Taking Market Share: the US apparel market is growing under 2% a year. When Aritzia's US sales grow 60% while Lululemon's Americas sales fall 8% and Nike North America grows 2%, Aritzia is winning customers while bigger competitors stall.
  • Still small: the US women's apparel market is about US$196 billion. Aritzia's US business is on track for roughly US$2.3 billion this year, about 1.2% of the market (my estimate).
  • Room to build: Aritzia had 76 US boutiques at the end of Q1, mostly in major metros like New York, Los Angeles and Chicago, and sees room for 180 to 200. A standard boutique does about C$10M a year in sales, costs about C$4M to open and has been paying back in under a year, so expansion funds itself. Filling the remaining 100+ sites would add roughly C$1B or more of yearly store sales (my estimate), before any lift to online.
  • Stores lift online sales: management says new boutiques build brand awareness that carries online. Digital revenue grew 68% last quarter.
  • A second customer, men: see below.
  • A plan to double: the five-year plan released Oct 27 targets C$9.5–10.5B of revenue by fiscal 2032 (15–17% a year), about 220 boutiques in total (146 today), a 21% adjusted EBITDA margin, up to C$500M from new countries, and about C$2B of buybacks.

05Men are the next customer

Almost all of Aritzia's growth so far has come from one customer: women. Menswear opens a second market, and the groundwork is already laid.

  • The foothold: in 2021 Aritzia bought 75% of Reigning Champ, a Vancouver premium athletic-wear brand, for about C$63M. Aritzia founder Brian Hill said at the time that men's “will become a meaningful part of Aritzia's platform.” Reigning Champ had four boutiques as of May 2026.
  • The build-out: in early 2026 Aritzia was hiring a Head of Design for Aritzia Men's, describing the company as “accelerating its expansion into menswear” and calling it “a high growth category.”
  • The plan: the five-year plan expects new product categories to add about C$1.5B of revenue by fiscal 2032, by expanding “the breadth of its assortment to capture additional share of closet.” The plan doesn't break out menswear, so I treat it as upside rather than part of my base case.

Why it matters: the same playbook of premium product, elevated stores and service, and fair prices has room to work again on a second customer. If it does, the runway gets longer using the stores and audience Aritzia already has.

The risk: many women's brands have struggled to make menswear work, and a weak launch would dilute the brand. I'll watch how the men's line is merchandised and whether it gets its own space in the boutiques.

My sell triggers

I wrote these down in advance so I'm not deciding under pressure.

  1. Thesis break: US revenue growth below 15% for two quarters in a row, or comparable sales turning negative.
  2. Moat break: full-year adjusted gross margin back below 45% (this year is guided to about 47.4%), or margin down from a year earlier for two quarters in a row. Either would signal the brand has to discount to sell. Single quarters swing with the seasons, so I don’t judge one quarter alone.
  3. Store economics break: new boutiques paying back slower than 18 months. Today they pay for themselves in under a year.
  4. Position too large: above 20% of my portfolio, I trim back toward 15% whatever the outlook.

A falling share price alone is not a sell trigger. If the business keeps hitting its numbers, a lower price makes the stock better value, not worse.

Position and catalysts

Aritzia is about 14.0% of my portfolio, my largest single stock and the top of the 10–15% I allow for any one company. My average cost is C$122.91. It's TSX-listed, so it doesn't overlap with US index funds, and I own it in Canadian dollars with no currency conversion.

Rules I follow

  1. No single stock above 15% at the time of purchase.
  2. Trim any position that grows above 20% of the portfolio.
  3. Add only on a pullback while the thesis is intact, never after a run-up.
  4. No trades within three days before or after publishing a report.

Catalysts to watch

  • Q3 FY27 results (Sep–Nov 2026), expected in early January 2027: does growth beat the 23–27% guidance?
  • Holiday season, Aritzia's biggest selling period, and a read on consumer spending.
  • New US boutiques and the first international markets under the five-year plan.
